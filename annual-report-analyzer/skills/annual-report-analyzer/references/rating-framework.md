# Rating Framework: Buy / Hold / Sell

A rating compresses a lot of analysis into one word, so it needs a consistent logic. This framework keeps ratings defensible and comparable from memo to memo.

## What each rating means

Ratings apply to a stated horizon (default 12 months) and are relative to the expected return of a broad equity index, adjusted for risk.

- **Buy**: Expected total return is meaningfully above what the market offers, and the thesis is supported by evidence that does not depend on everything going right. Rough guide: 15% or more upside to a reasonable estimate of value, with a downside case that is tolerable.
- **Hold**: Expected return is roughly in line with the market, or attractive quality is offset by a full price, or the outcome is too uncertain to underwrite either way. Rough guide: within about ±10-15% of estimated value.
- **Sell**: Expected return is meaningfully below the market, or downside risk is not compensated by the price. Rough guide: 15% or more downside to estimated value, or a deteriorating business the market has not priced in.

The percentages are guides, not mechanical rules. Explain any departure.

## The four questions

Answer each explicitly before choosing a rating. In the memo, each question gets its own subtitle in the Recommendation section, with the answer first and then the reasoning.

1. **Quality**: Is this a good business? Look at returns on capital, margin stability, competitive position, and cash generation.
2. **Trajectory**: Is it getting better or worse? Look at growth, margin direction, market share, and management's execution against past promises.
3. **Price**: What is the market paying, and what does that imply? Compare to history, peers, and a rough estimate of intrinsic value.
4. **Risk**: What could go wrong, how likely is it, and how bad? Include balance sheet fragility, regulatory or litigation exposure, concentration, and disruption.

## Converting analysis into a rating

| Quality / Trajectory | Price cheap vs value | Price fair | Price rich |
|----------------------|---------------------|-----------|-----------|
| Strong and improving | **Buy** | Buy or Hold | Hold |
| Strong but stable or slowing | Buy | Hold | Hold or Sell |
| Mediocre or mixed | Buy only with a clear catalyst; else Hold | Hold | Sell |
| Weak or deteriorating | Hold (price may already reflect it) | Sell | Sell |

Adjust one notch toward caution when:
- The balance sheet cannot absorb a downturn
- The thesis depends on a single unproven event
- Data quality is poor, or the filing has accounting red flags
- You lack independent confirmation of management's key claims

## Valuation range

The price target comes from the Scenario Analysis: a probability-weighted price across bull, base, and bear cases. Present it with the range of the three cases so it does not read as false precision. Acceptable methods, in order of preference for most companies:

1. **Multiple-based**: apply a defensible multiple (based on the company's history and peers) to a forward earnings, cash flow, or EBITDA estimate.
2. **Simple DCF or FCF yield**: state growth, margin, and discount rate assumptions in plain terms, and show how sensitive the result is to them.
3. **Sum of the parts** for conglomerates with very different segments.

Present **bull / base / bear** cases, each with a probability (summing to 100%), next-year revenue and EPS growth, forward EPS, a P/E multiple, and the price that follows (forward EPS x P/E). The target is the sum of probability x price; show the arithmetic. If any inputs are estimates you have assembled from web sources, flag them as estimates and name the source.

## Confidence and what would change the view

End the recommendation with:

- **Confidence**: High, Medium, or Low, and the main reason for it (usually data quality or the size of the uncertainty).
- **What would make me more positive** and **what would make me more negative**: two or three concrete, observable developments each (for example, "operating margin recovering above 20%", or "loss of the top customer"). This turns the memo into something the reader can monitor.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a great company as a Buy at any price, or a beaten-down stock as a Buy simply because it fell.
- Anchoring on management's adjusted numbers without checking the GAAP figures.
- Presenting a rating that the body of the memo does not support. The reader should reach the same conclusion from the evidence.
- Hiding the bear case. If the strongest counterargument is not in the memo, the rating is not credible.
- Using stale prices. State the price date and recompute upside if the memo is reused later.
